Diegetic interfaces are user interfaces that exist as part of the virtual world itself. Compared to traditional GUI elements, such as menus, they often feel more immersive and engaging, as they reinforce the user’s sense of presence. Diegetic interfaces can also help reduce motion sickness, especially when used instead of GUIs that are pinned to the user’s field of view (see [Motion Sickness](Motion-Sickness.md) and [GUI](Graphical-User-Interface.md)). However, they are often more complex to use, and studies have shown that users may take longer to complete tasks with diegetic interfaces than with non-diegetic ones. For shorter applications or training simulations, it can therefore be beneficial to prioritise simple, intuitive diegetic interactions or rely on non-diegetic interfaces to reduce complexity and mental load. \[[15](References.md#ref-15), [28](References.md#ref-28), [36](References.md#ref-36)\]

* **Favour Intuitive Design:** Diegetic objects and interactions should closely resemble their real-world counterparts. When interactions behave in familiar ways, users can rely on their real-world knowledge and expectations, reducing the need for explicit instructions. This makes interactions easier to understand and helps users feel more confident and comfortable within the environment. \[[28](References.md#ref-28), [36](References.md#ref-36)\]
* **Favour Simple Design:** While diegetic interactions can enhance immersion and presence, they can also increase cognitive load, frustration, and task difficulty if they are overly complex. To minimise these negative effects, interactions should be kept as simple as possible. Straightforward actions, such as a single controller button press or an intuitive hand gesture, can preserve immersion without overwhelming the user. \[[28](References.md#ref-28), [36](References.md#ref-36)\]
* **Provide Proper Instruction:** Even when interactions are intuitive and simple, some users may still struggle, especially if they are unfamiliar with XR. Users may not immediately know which interactions are possible or how to perform them. To support them, clear instructions should be provided through onboarding methods (see [Onboarding](Onboarding.md)), contextual prompts, instruction panels, or temporary button highlights (e.g. a UI element that appears when the user approaches or interacts with an object). \[[28](References.md#ref-28), [36](References.md#ref-36)\]
* **Provide Appropriate Feedback:** Clear feedback is essential when interacting with diegetic interfaces. Without sufficient feedback, users may feel confused or uncertain about whether their interaction was successful. Feedback can be provided through animations (e.g. a diegetic button moving when pressed or a lever responding to being pulled), sound effects, controller vibrations, or a combination of these (see [Interaction Feedback](Interaction-Feedback.md)). Well-designed feedback not only improves usability but also strengthens the user’s sense of presence and immersion. A lack of feedback, or feedback that is too subtle, can make interactions feel unnatural and contribute to overconfident or unrealistic behaviour (see [Super Soldier Syndrome](Super-Soldier-Syndrome.md)). \[[15](References.md#ref-15), [36](References.md#ref-36)\]
* **Favour Appropriate Cues:** Users need to be able to distinguish interactive objects from non-interactive ones. Visual cues can help communicate interactivity, such as making objects slightly brighter or less saturated, adding subtle outlines, increasing detail compared to surrounding objects, or using gaze- or controller-based highlights. In some cases, additional 2D elements such as icons, markers, or short text prompts can be used to draw attention, as long as they do not break immersion or clutter the scene. \[[6](References.md#ref-6), [13](References.md#ref-13)\]
* **Favour Proper Placement:** Diegetic objects are often placed alongside non-interactable objects, which can make them harder to identify. In addition to using visual cues, careful placement plays an important role. Interactive objects should be clearly visible, easy to reach, and not obstructed by other elements in the environment. Objects that are hidden, placed too far away, or blocked by non-interactive objects are more likely to be overlooked or ignored by the user. \[[13](References.md#ref-13)\]
