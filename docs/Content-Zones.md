Where content is placed in an XR application has a strong influence on how comfortable it is to use and how well it is perceived. A related concept is proxemics, which describes how the space around a person is perceived and used. The following sections explain the concept and the content zones derived from it for VR.

#### Proxemics:

* **Understand Proxemics:** Edward T. Hall divided the space around people into four interpersonal zones. The intimate distance (0 to 45 cm) is reserved for close relationships and physical contact. The personal distance (45 cm to 1.2 m) is used for conversations with friends and everyday social exchanges. The social distance (1.2 m to 3.6 m) is maintained during formal or professional interactions. The public distance (3.6 m and beyond) is typically kept during lectures or in public. \[[25](References.md#ref-25)\]
* **Consider Social and Cultural Context:** These interpersonal zones depend on social bonds and cultural norms. \[[25](References.md#ref-25)\]
* **Mind the Placement of Objects and Agents:** Proxemics can significantly affect how a product is perceived and used. An example from robotics: a jogging drone that flies behind a person may make them feel chased, which can lower the enjoyment of jogging, while a drone that flies in front may make them feel like the chaser, which can increase motivation. Depending on the placement, positive or negative reactions can be elicited, and the acceptance of the robot can increase or decrease. \[[3](References.md#ref-3)\]
* **Expect Proxemics to Apply in VR:** Although proxemics were first defined for physical space, they apply in a similar pattern to VR, even though the distances between the zones might vary. \[[37](References.md#ref-37)\]

#### Content Zones in VR:

Mike Alger developed a concept analogous to proxemics that divides the space within the virtual world into zones. It assumes that users are seated in a non-rotating chair. Alger used measurements of perception depth at different distances in VR, the comfortable range in which people can rotate their heads, and the general field of view of the human eyes to define four zones. \[[1](References.md#ref-1)\]

* **No-No Zone:** This zone comprises the area directly around the user's head at a radius of about 0.5 meters, because persistent content begins to be uncomfortable at this distance. No permanent interface elements should be placed here. \[[1](References.md#ref-1)\]
* **Peripheral Zone:** This zone comprises the areas to the left and right of the user's sight. People can see elements in their periphery, but it might strain them, which makes this zone inappropriate for content that needs to be seen on a regular basis. \[[1](References.md#ref-1)\]
* **Curiosity Zone:** This zone extends from the peripheral zone to the back of the user. Content here can only be seen with active rotation, e.g. when a user is curious about what is behind them. \[[1](References.md#ref-1)\]
* **Content Zone:** This is the area that users can view comfortably right in front of them, up to around 20 m. Content beyond this distance loses the benefit of depth perception and is therefore not suitable to convey information. Important or persistent UI elements and text should generally be placed within this comfortable viewing zone. \[[1](References.md#ref-1)\]
