Visual fatigue is a form of mental or physical exhaustion resulting from excessive exertion of the visual system and has been linked to the use of digital systems, especially Head-Mounted-Displays (HMDs). The most common symptoms of visual fatigue include blurred vision, dry eyes, headaches, eyestrain, and neck and shoulder pain. Research suggests that people under 40 are more affected by visual fatigue. Additionally, studies have shown that visual fatigue is associated with cognitive load. An increase in visual fatigue can negatively affect users' attention and concentration, which is a major issue for longer training sessions. Thus, it is important to make the application as comfortable as possible for the user so they can gain the necessary information and experience from it. \[[41, 42, 43, 53](https://github.com/theorchestrator/eac-ux-guidelines/blob/main/Guidelines/References.md)\]

* **Favour Dark Displayed Stimuli:** Various research has shown that dark mode, i.e. bright letters on a dark background, causes significantly less eye strain than light mode, i.e. dark letters on a bright background. Reducing overall visual brightness can also help limit visual fatigue. \[[17, 42](https://github.com/theorchestrator/eac-ux-guidelines/blob/main/Guidelines/References.md)\]
* **Maintain Consistent Brightness Between Scenes:** Keeping brightness levels stable across different scenes to avoid a noticeable change in brightness that could create unnecessary discomfort, especially during longer sessions. \[[41](https://github.com/theorchestrator/eac-ux-guidelines/blob/main/Guidelines/References.md)\]
* **Favour Low-Saturated Colours:** Low-saturated colours are softer and less visually demanding (see [Colours](https://github.com/theorchestrator/eac-ux-guidelines/blob/main/Guidelines/Visual-Design-&-Presentation/Colours.md)), making them helpful in reducing eye fatigue over longer use. They work especially well in larger, attention-heavy areas such as panels, menus, or text backgrounds, where intense colours can quickly become tiring. \[[31, 33, 42, 43, 53](https://github.com/theorchestrator/eac-ux-guidelines/blob/main/Guidelines/References.md)\]
* **Favour Low-Luminescent Colours:** High-luminescent colours are colours that have a natural brightness (see [Colours](https://github.com/theorchestrator/eac-ux-guidelines/blob/main/Guidelines/Visual-Design-&-Presentation/Colours.md)). These colours can feel intense and tiring, especially when they sit close to the user’s eyes in an HMD. It is recommended to use these colours sparingly, in particular for larger areas that capture the user's attention for a longer period, such as text backgrounds. \[[31, 33, 42, 43, 53](https://github.com/theorchestrator/eac-ux-guidelines/blob/main/Guidelines/References.md)\]
* **Avoid Frequent Colour Changes:** Sudden and frequent colour changes can lead to a strain on the visual system. The more frequent the colour change, the higher the visual fatigue. \[[33, 42, 43](https://github.com/theorchestrator/eac-ux-guidelines/blob/main/Guidelines/References.md)\]
* **Avoid Unnatural Depth Cues:** Depth cues that seem unnatural or contradict, e.g. sharp textures for objects in the far distance or a texture gradient that is inconsistent with the object rotation, can lead to visual discomfort. To minimise this effect and to help with orientation, it is recommended to reproduce gradients that mimic the perception of the real world. \[[31, 41](https://github.com/theorchestrator/eac-ux-guidelines/blob/main/Guidelines/References.md)\]
* **Apply Motion Parallax for Head Movements:** Subtle, natural motion parallax cues generated by head movement can support the user’s sense of stability and reduce discomfort. Slow and consistent parallax responses help mitigate visual fatigue by aligning the virtual world more closely with real-world visual expectations. \[[31, 41](https://github.com/theorchestrator/eac-ux-guidelines/blob/main/Guidelines/References.md)\]
* **Avoid Vertical Parallax:** Research suggests that vertical parallax, along with abrupt discontinuities in motion parallax, can increase visual fatigue. Keeping parallax effects subtle and consistent supports a more comfortable viewing experience. \[[31, 41](https://github.com/theorchestrator/eac-ux-guidelines/blob/main/Guidelines/References.md)\]
* **Ease the Mental Workload:** Excessive mental workload can lead to an increase in visual fatigue. Clear guidance, such as virtual assistants, visual cues, and intuitive feedback, could help users understand how to complete tasks without becoming overwhelmed. Giving users time to orient themselves when entering the virtual environment also reduces pressure and contributes to a smoother experience. The NASA TLX can be used to test the mental workload of the application (see [User Comfort Questionnaires](https://github.com/theorchestrator/eac-ux-guidelines/blob/main/Guidelines/User-Comfort-Questionnaires.md)) \[[41, 42](https://github.com/theorchestrator/eac-ux-guidelines/blob/main/Guidelines/References.md)\]
* **Avoid Dynamism in Videos:** Fast-paced and dynamic videos in a virtual environment can lead to an increase in visual fatigue for users. Slower and calmer videos are recommended in particular for instruction videos. \[[42](https://github.com/theorchestrator/eac-ux-guidelines/blob/main/Guidelines/References.md)\]
* **Allow Small Breaks:** The longer the duration of the application goes on, the more likely it is that the user can experience visual fatigue. Research has shown that visual fatigue is common after 20 minutes of use, and some symptoms can already appear after 10 minutes. Encouraging short breaks roughly every 15 minutes helps users reset, reduces strain, and normalises the experience of needing rest while using VR. \[[41, 42](https://github.com/theorchestrator/eac-ux-guidelines/blob/main/Guidelines/References.md)\]
